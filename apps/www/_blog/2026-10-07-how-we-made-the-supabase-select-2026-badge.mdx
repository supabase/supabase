[Supabase Select](https://select.supabase.com/) is our developer conference, and this year it took place in San Francisco on October 2. Rather than yet another name tag destined for the trash, every attendee received a programmable badge they can keep hacking on long after the conference.

During the conference it was an animated name tag with the schedule and a couple of games on it. Since then, people have turned it into a [live flight tracker](https://x.com/jsmillerdev/status/2107160820455035216), an [arcade game about dead tuples](https://x.com/LFGAmy/status/2106146139166879956), and a [language model](https://x.com/maddiedreese/status/2106147063759278250) that runs entirely on the badge.

Here’s how we went about building the badges, and some ideas to push it even further.

## What's inside

The badge is based on the [Pimoroni Tufty 2350](https://shop.pimoroni.com/products/tufty-2350) and ships with:

- An RP2350 microcontroller
- A color screen
- A rechargeable battery
- Onboard Wi-Fi and Bluetooth
- Five physical buttons you can map to anything

## Making a thousand of them

Our first iteration was a board with a Raspberry Pi Pico, a screen, and a battery, attached so you could pull them apart and use them as a starter kit once the conference was over. It was a great solution for building a modular conference badge, but assembling a thousand of them by hand was not feasible.

<Img
  alt="The prototype badge: a Raspberry Pi Pico 2 W, a screen, and a battery holder on one board"

With our next iteration, we went with [Pimoroni](https://shop.pimoroni.com), who build similar hardware - screen, battery and programmable microcontroller - but on a single board, greatly reducing the complexity of assembly.

## Getting 800 badges ready

The badges arrived two weeks before Select: 800 of them, in 20 boxes of 40, with flat batteries.

Charging 800 badges turned into a fun math problem. Each badge draws less than half an amp, so we didn't need anything fancy: two 30-port USB charging hubs, the kind schools use to charge iPads overnight, and a pile of the cheapest cables we could find. A full charge takes about three hours. With 60 ports, that's 14 batches. Add an hour per batch for plugging, unplugging, and powering down, and it works out to about 56 hours of charging.

The schedule app was meant to pull live updates over Wi-Fi, but the venue network wasn't going to cooperate. A laptop could stay connected from the far end of the parking lot, while the badges couldn't connect at all, and 800 badges sharing the 2.4 GHz band with every neighboring network was never going to be reliable. So the badges went offline-first. We set up our own router broadcasting the network name already saved on the badges, connected it to a cellular hotspot, and synced every badge’s schedule before the doors opened. Open the schedule app while connected, and it pulls the latest version.

## What people have built so far

Check out these cool projects built by people at Select and others watching from home:

- [Select Sky](https://x.com/jsmillerdev/status/2107160820455035216)

  Joey (@jsmillerdev) built a live flight tracker with a radar view, individual flight tracking, and details like altitude and speed.

- [A pocket food finder](https://x.com/Mike_demo/status/2106230900371382485)

  Mike Demo (@Mike_demo) made a badge app that helps attendees find places to eat, with filters for vegan, vegetarian, gluten-free, kosher, and halal options. He wasn't at Select and joined in remotely.

- [A game about collecting music notes](https://x.com/tiff_ishh/status/2107501144704761934)

  Tiffany Collins (@tiff_ishh) turned hers into a game where the Tenor logo collects notes while dodging an angry composer.

- [Dead Tuples](https://x.com/LFGAmy/status/2106146139166879956)

  Amy Mayernik (@LFGAmy) watched the keynote from home and turned the OrioleDB section into an arcade game, where you vacuum dead rows before the table fills up and then unlock an OrioleDB mode that gives you a break from cleanup.

- [A language model running locally](https://x.com/maddiedreese/status/2106147063759278250)

  Maddie D. Reese (@maddiedreese) fit a tiny story-generation model onto the badge with an on-screen keyboard to prompt it, running entirely on the device.

- [Muse on a badge](https://x.com/wobsoriano/status/2106252508587073877)

  Robert Soriano (@wobsoriano) got home and had his badge running Muse before the week was out.

There are many more in the [badge showcase](https://badge.select/showcase), and every one of them started from the same hardware and ended up somewhere we wouldn't have thought to go.

## Hack your badge

If you can build a web or mobile app, you already know most of what it takes to put a screen on your desk, or a sensor in your room that does something useful when a reading changes. The hardware is inexpensive, and was not affected by the recent price increase of memory - Raspberry Pi raised the price of their computers several times, while microcontrollers remained the same price. With a tool like Claude Code, a first hardware project becomes an afternoon spent mostly on deciding what to make.

Everything you need is at [badge.select](https://badge.select/):

- If you have a physical badge, the [first app guide](https://badge.select/guides/first-program) walks you through adding your own app over USB.
- If you want a coding agent to help, the [badge project kit](https://badge.select/guides/code-with-an-agent) gives Claude Code the API reference and instructions it needs. You can try something like: "Read the project instructions and badge API reference. Build a pomodoro timer with a large countdown. Use A to start or pause, B to reset, and C to switch between work and break sessions."
- If you weren't at Select, the [virtual badge](https://badge.select/virtual) and the [Make editor](https://badge.select/make) let you build in your browser.
- If you want to go further, replace the firmware and work in C or Rust. There's already [a guide](https://badge.select/guides/apple-ii) for turning the badge into an Apple IIe emulator, with a physical USB keyboard and BASIC programs running on the badge itself.

Good first projects are small and useful: a clock with exactly the display you want, a pomodoro timer beside your keyboard, a display that reads from your Supabase project over Wi-Fi, or a light that turns on when your coding agent needs you. With Supabase Realtime, the badge can react the moment something changes in your app. Your first change can be as small as making one button do something different.
